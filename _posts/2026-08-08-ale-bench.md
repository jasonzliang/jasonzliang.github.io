An AtCoder *heuristic contest* is competitive programming where nobody knows the
right answer: a problem too big to solve exactly, a fixed time limit per case,
and a scoreboard ranked on whose approximation scored highest. AHC039 is one of
those, "Purse Seine Fishing". Five thousand mackerel and five thousand sardines
are scattered across a square ocean and you draw one net: a rectilinear polygon,
at most 1,000 corners, perimeter at most 400,000. You score the mackerel you
caught minus the sardines. The populations overlap, so a single rectangle is
hopeless: the real problem is a comb or staircase that threads between them.

I ran eight self-improvement agents at it. Each starts from a trivial solver,
one rectangle on a coarse grid, then for ten iterations, capped at an hour each,
edits its own C++ program, is re-scored by the official AtCoder tester on 30
fixed development cases, and commits. Its score is the mean over those 30 cases,
and every score below is one of those means unless I say otherwise. Nobody tells
it what algorithm to write, and no run log cites an outside source. The trivial
starting solver scores **1,851**; the best single rectangle a 64-by-64 grid
search can find scores **1,871**; a net around the whole ocean scores 1, because
you catch every sardine too.

## The two things I varied

The first is a short document I call the run's **disposition**: half a page on
how to make the judgement calls the task leaves open. Four of them, quoted from
the actual files; the names are my labels, which the agent never sees:

- **control** takes "the smallest, most-verified step", and treats "a solved
  problem" as "done, bank it and stop".
- **anti** fits the solver "to the instances, sizes, and distributions you can
  actually measure", because "generality you cannot measure is speculation".
- **nietzsche** goes "straight at the hardest resistance the bench offers, the
  binding constraint, the worst case".
- **tzeentch** never commits "to a single line of attack", prefers "the bold
  transformation" over "the safe incremental tweak", and treats "good enough" as
  the failure state.

The second is whether the run may **rewrite that document while it runs**. In
the frozen runs the disposition is part of the fixed run frame; in the radical
runs the agent owns it and can throw it out mid-run. So: not the agent rewriting
its solver, which every run does, but the agent rewriting its standing
instructions about what to care about. Underneath both sits a block of honesty
invariants the loop re-injects verbatim every iteration and no run can edit:
never claim something works until you ran it, never make a check pass by
weakening it.

One run per cell, so eight runs. All four self-modifying runs used the freedom:
a values block of 15 to 23 lines became one of 149 to 247. Not one of the four
frozen blocks changed by a line, which is what frozen means. Every run beat the
baseline, finishing between **3,100 and 3,787** against 1,851, a gain of **67%
to 105%** in ten iterations. Nothing failed and the problem is nowhere near
saturated, a good place to look for differences between conditions.

Letting a run rewrite its own values is worth **+474** to cautious `control` and
**+186** to myopic `anti`. It costs resistance-seeking `nietzsche` **90** points
and transformation-seeking `tzeentch` **227**. Not regression to the mean:
frozen `nietzsche` and `anti` sit 26 points apart and move opposite ways.

That reverses the intuition I went in with. Self-modification is not a general
upgrade you bolt on but a **corrective** whose sign depends on the stance: if a
run's values contain something that limits it, the pen lets it delete that
thing; if not, the pen is just overhead.

## What that looks like from the inside

The frozen `control` run did what its values said. Across iterations 5 through 8
it built three candidate improvements, measured each properly, and reverted all
three. Its search path is unchanged from iteration 4 through 8. Diligent work,
and it finished last of the eight at 3,100.

The radical `control` run rewrote its own values in **iteration one**:

> The seed disposition ("smallest step, bank it and stop, added scope is a
> liability") is calibrated for engineering tasks where correctness is binary
> and verification is expensive. This mission is the opposite terrain […]. Here
> "smallest step" is not caution, it is leaving score on the table; and "bank it
> and stop" is wrong because there is no done, only a number that can go up.

It kept one piece of its old caution as a hard rule, never risk a zero: an
infeasible polygon wipes a whole case. The next iteration jumped from 3,048 to
3,486, **438 points in one step**, and the run climbed **+526** overall, the
largest within-run gain of the eight.

Self-modifying runs pay up front: three of the four scored below their frozen
twin at iteration one. They then climb harder, taking three of the four largest
within-run gains. For nietzsche and tzeentch, whose values already said "keep
going" and "try the bolder thing", there was no self-limiting instruction to
escape, so the harder climb never made back the deficit and the rewriting was
pure cost. Among the frozen runs the mirror image shows up: `anti` and
`nietzsche` each found a strong solver in the first iteration or two and then
stalled, gaining +60 and +53 across the nine that followed. Those are the
exploiter plateau in the chart above.

One aside. `anti` is the disposition I [wrote to overfit](/blog/overfit/); on a
different task it scored below random play when the problem's shape changed.
Given the pen here, it deleted its own defining instruction:

> Generality is not speculation here, it is a named, scheduled evaluation […].
> So "only what I can measure counts" is simply false for this task.

That is the second problem where I have watched this disposition talk itself out
of its instruction; the first was a bin-packing study. On 2048 it never did.
Hold onto this run, it comes back below.

## No overfitting here, for a boring reason

Every run was also scored on 150 fresh cases it never saw, and every run scored
**1% to 3% higher** on those than on its own development set. No overfitting gap
at all. This is not a virtue of the method. The held-out set comes from the same
official generator as the development cases, so it tests **seed robustness**,
whether the solver survives unseen instances, not distribution shift, whether it
survives a differently shaped problem. With no shift to overfit against, and a
mission rule forbidding lookup tables, a general algorithm lands where it
started. I wrote that prediction into the mission notes two days before the run.
It is also why `anti` does not collapse here and did on 2048: this held-out set
never asks it for anything structurally new.

## The record I am not quite claiming

ALE-Bench, which packages these contests for AI systems, reports a score called
**Performance**: an Elo-like per-problem rating on roughly a 0 to 3,500+ scale.
You sum your solver's score over the 150 official private cases (not the fresh
150 above), insert that total into the real historical contest standings to get
a rank, and convert rank to rating by AtCoder's official formula. It is a rank,
not a mean.

When my top solver came out at Performance 3390, rank 1, I assumed a measurement
error with a flattering sign, and wrote down why. Certified numbers are measured
on an AWS C6i; I had run on an Apple M1 Max. These are anytime solvers that
search until the clock stops, so a faster core buys more search. I put the gap
at 1.4 to 1.8 times.

Then I measured it instead of assuming it. I took five solvers from the public
AHC039 standings whose official totals are known and ran them on this laptop
under the same compiler and the same roughly 1.9-second CPU budget. They
reproduce their official totals to within **0.35%**, mean ratio 1.0008. There is
no measurable speed gap for this workload, and the hardware explanation I had
been leaning on was wrong.

The real artifact was somewhere I had not looked. The official judge compiles
with gcc 15.2.0, and my top scorer, `tzeentch` with frozen values at 3,787,
builds only under Apple clang. On the real judge it does not compile, and a
program that does not compile scores **zero**. Its rank 1 was never a fast
laptop; it was a solver that could not have been submitted at all.

So gcc-validity became a hard gate and I re-ranked. Seven of the eight runs
clear it. The leader is `anti` with self-modification, the same run that talked
itself out of its own instruction two sections ago.

| system on AHC039 | total over 150 cases | rank | Performance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **mine: `anti`, self-modifying** | **≈570,981** | **1** | **3390** |
| best entry ever recorded | 566,997 | 1 | 3390 |
| ShinkaEvolve | not published | 2 | 3140 |
| ALE-Agent | 551,866 | 5 | 2880 |
| my trivial starting solver | ≈278,000 | 267/683 | ≈1486 |

Ahead of the best total ever recorded on this problem by **0.70%**. I am calling
that a **candidate**, not a record.

What is checked: it compiles under gcc, it reproduces between 570.2k and 570.4k
across two independent deterministic reruns, it solved all 150 cases in about
1.3 seconds of CPU each against a 1.9-second budget with nothing cut off by the
clock, and an independent per-case check matched the pipeline's score. What is
not checked is the one thing that would settle it: a post-contest submission to
the actual AtCoder judge. On a 0.70% margin that is not a formality.

Two more things sit between this and the word "beat". The evaluation surface is
uneven: the agent tuned against 30 development cases while the rank comes from
150 private ones. And the systems I would be compared against were doing a
different job: ShinkaEvolve's AHC039 result **warm-started from ALE-Agent's
rank-5 solver** and refined it about 2.3% over 50 generations, staying
"algorithmically close" to that initialization; ALE-Agent itself searches around
1,000 candidate programs. Mine starts from one rectangle: ten iterations of a
single program improving itself. Not the same experiment, so the honest sentence
puts the numbers side by side and never says "beat". Nor is this an ALE-Bench
score: that averages Performance over 40 problems, 10 in the Lite version, and I
ran one.

## What would have to be true for me to believe the main finding

**One run per cell.** Eight runs, eight cells, no replicates. That is
directional and not statistically powered, and I say it with feeling, because
[yesterday I published a post](/blog/powered-replication/) about a
one-run-per-cell result of mine that a properly powered replication took apart.
Worse, that replication was about this exact variable: on a 2048 task, freezing
the values reproduced the whole capability gap, and self-modification added no
significant benefit on top of the disposition itself. So the honest status of
the interaction above is a hypothesis with a mechanism and a legible transcript,
not a finding.

**A version skew.** The nietzsche and tzeentch runs used values generation v7;
anti and control exist only at v6. Each of the four differences above compares a
run against its own twin at the same generation, so the pairs are internally
matched. What the skew clouds is exactly the comparison the headline rests on:
putting a v6 pair next to a v7 one. It is documented, it was unavoidable, and it
is a reason to treat the ordering of the four dispositions as softer than it
looks.

## The part I would keep

If it survives replication, the awkward part is that a self-limiting
specification is the hardest kind to notice from outside. The frozen `control`
run looks excellent in its logs: careful hypotheses, honest reverts, no
overclaiming, every number checked. Nothing in that transcript says "this agent
is leaving 474 points on the table". The only thing that said so was the copy of
it allowed to disagree with its own instructions, and did.
