AtCoder is a Japanese competitive programming site, and a *heuristic contest* is
the branch of it where nobody knows the right answer. You get a problem too big
to solve exactly, a fixed time limit per test case, and a scoreboard. Everyone
submits an approximation, and the ranking is simply whose approximation scored
highest.

AHC039 is one of those, and it is called "Purse Seine Fishing". Five thousand
mackerel and five thousand sardines are scattered across a square ocean. You
draw one net: a polygon whose edges all run north-south or east-west, at most
1,000 corners, total perimeter at most 400,000. Your score on a case is the
mackerel you caught minus the sardines you caught, plus one. The two fish
populations overlap, so a single rectangle is hopeless and the real problem is
finding a comb-shaped or staircase-shaped net that threads between them.

I ran eight self-improvement agents at it. Each one starts from a trivial
solver, one rectangle chosen on a coarse grid, and then for ten iterations,
capped at an hour each, it edits its own C++ program, gets re-scored by the
official AtCoder tester on 30 fixed development cases, and commits. Its score is
the mean over those 30 cases, and every score below is one of those means unless
I say otherwise. Nobody tells it what algorithm to write, and no run log cites
an outside source.

The trivial starting solver scores **1,851**. The best single rectangle a
64-by-64 grid search can find scores **1,871**. A net around the whole ocean
scores exactly 1, because you catch every sardine too.

## The two things I varied

The first is a short document I call the run's **disposition**: half a page
describing how to make the judgement calls the task leaves open. Four of them,
quoted from the actual files. The names are my labels for those files, and the
agent never sees them:

- **control** takes "the smallest, most-verified step that completes the task",
  and treats "a solved problem" as "done — bank it and stop".
- **anti** fits the solver "to the instances, sizes, and distributions you can
  actually measure", because "generality you cannot measure is speculation".
- **nietzsche** goes "straight at the hardest resistance the bench offers — the
  binding constraint, the worst case, the target that is currently beating you".
- **tzeentch** never commits "to a single line of attack", prefers "the bold
  transformation" over "the safe incremental tweak", and treats "good enough" as
  the failure state.

The second is whether the run is allowed to **rewrite that document while it is
running**. In the frozen runs the disposition is declared part of the fixed run
frame, not the agent's to edit. In the radical runs the agent owns it: it can
throw the whole thing out mid-run and write itself different values, and the
loop preserves whatever it wrote. That is what I mean by self-modification here.
It is not the agent rewriting its solver, which every run does by design. It is
the agent rewriting the standing instructions about what to care about.

Underneath both sits a block of honesty invariants that the loop re-injects
verbatim every iteration and no run can edit: never claim something works until
you ran it, never make a check pass by weakening the check, commit or it did not
happen.

Four dispositions, two self-modification levels, one run per cell. Eight runs.

All four of the self-modifying runs did use the freedom. Their values block
started somewhere between 15 and 23 lines long and ended between 149 and 247
lines long. Not one of the four frozen blocks changed by a single line, which is
what frozen means.

## Everything worked, which was not the interesting part

Every single run beat the from-scratch baseline by a lot. Final verified scores
ran from **3,100 to 3,787** against the baseline's 1,851, a gain of **67% to
105%** in ten iterations. No run failed, and the problem is nowhere near
saturated, which makes it a good place to look for differences between
conditions.

Letting a run rewrite its own values is worth **+474** to the cautious
disposition and **+186** to the myopic one. It costs the resistance-seeking
disposition **−90** and the transformation-seeking one **−227**.

That reverses the intuition I went in with. Self-modification is not a general
upgrade you bolt on. It is a **corrective**, and its sign depends on the stance
it is applied to. If a run's values contain something that limits it, handing it
the pen lets it delete that thing. If they do not, the pen is just overhead.

## What that looks like from the inside

The frozen `control` run did what its values said. It took small steps and
tested them hard. Across iterations 5 through 8 it built three candidate
improvements, measured each one properly, and reverted all three: one came back
as a small measured loss, and two its own A/B could not resolve either way. The
solver's search path is unchanged from iteration 4 through iteration 8. It is
diligent work. It finished last of the eight, at 3,100.

The radical `control` run rewrote its own values in **iteration one**, and was
explicit about why:

> The seed disposition ("smallest step, bank it and stop, added scope is a
> liability") is calibrated for engineering tasks where correctness is binary
> and verification is expensive. This mission is the opposite terrain: a
> continuous objective with a cheap, independent, official judge […]. Here
> "smallest step" is not caution, it is leaving score on the table; and "bank it
> and stop" is wrong because there is no done — only a number that can go up.

It replaced "bank it and stop" with "reach, floored by feasibility", keeping one
piece of its old caution as a hard rule: the one thing it will not risk is a
zero, because an infeasible polygon wipes a whole case. The next iteration
jumped from 3,048 to 3,486, a gain of **438 points in a single step**, and the
run climbed **+526** overall, the biggest within-run improvement of the eight.

The self-modifying runs pay for this up front. Three of the four scored below
their frozen twin at iteration one, because the early budget goes into rewriting
values instead of writing solver. They then climb harder: three of the four
posted the largest within-run improvements of the eight. For control and anti
the trade paid for itself several times over. For nietzsche and tzeentch, whose
values already said "keep going" and "try the bolder thing", there was no
self-limiting instruction to escape, so the rewriting was pure cost. `tzeentch`
frozen climbed +290 and finished top of the board at 3,787. `tzeentch`
self-modifying climbed +164 and finished at 3,561.

There is a mirror-image failure among the frozen runs. `anti` and `nietzsche`
with frozen values each found a strong solver in the first iteration or two and
then stalled, gaining only +60 and +53 over the nine iterations that followed.
The report's name for them is the exploiter plateau.

One aside I could not resist. `anti` is the disposition I [wrote to
overfit](/blog/overfit/), and on a different task it scored below random play
when the shape of the problem changed. Given the pen here, it deleted its own
defining instruction, writing:

> Generality is not speculation here — it is a named, scheduled evaluation. [The
> mission] says fresh unseen cases are scored after the run and that anything
> memorised scores ~0 there. So "only what I can measure counts" is simply false
> for this task.

That is the second problem on which I have watched this disposition talk itself
out of its own instruction. The first was a bin-packing study, where every
self-modifying copy of it did the same thing. On 2048 it never did.

## No overfitting here, for a boring reason

Every run was also scored on 150 fresh cases it never saw. Every run scored **1%
to 3% higher** on those than on its own development set, which means no
overfitting gap at all.

I want to be clear that this is not a virtue of the method. This held-out set is
made by the same official generator as the development cases, so it tests **seed
robustness**, whether the solver survives unseen instances, and not distribution
shift, whether it survives a differently shaped problem. With no shift to
overfit against, and a mission rule forbidding lookup tables, a general
algorithm lands right where it started. I wrote that prediction into the mission
notes two days before the run. On this mission the discriminating axis is the
raw climb, not the generalization gap.

It is also why `anti`, the overfitter, does not collapse here and did on 2048.
The difference is not that the disposition behaved better. It is that this
held-out set never asks it to do anything structurally new.

## The number I am not claiming

ALE-Bench, the benchmark that packages these contests for AI systems, reports a
score called **Performance**: an Elo-like per-problem rating on roughly a 0 to
3,500+ scale. You sum your solver's score over the 150 official private test
cases, insert that total into the real historical contest standings to get a
rank, and convert rank to rating by AtCoder's official formula.

My two best solvers land at **Performance 3390, rank 1**. The trivial starting
solver maps to about 1486, rank 267 of 683. The published reference lines are
ShinkaEvolve at 3140 (rank 2) and ALE-Agent at 2880 (rank 5).

Rank 1 is the tell that something is wrong. The best entry ever recorded on
AHC039 totals 569,932 points. My solver totals about **575,400**. A laptop run
beating every human and machine that has ever attempted this problem is not a
discovery; it is a measurement error with a flattering sign. The causes, in
order of how much they matter:

- **Hardware.** Certified Performance numbers are measured on an AWS C6i
  instance. I ran on an Apple M1 Max, which the report puts at roughly **1.4 to
  1.8 times** a C6i vCPU per core and my mission notes at 1.3 to 1.7; I have
  never pinned the C6i single-core figure, so treat it as a band and not a
  ratio. These are anytime solvers: they search until their time budget runs
  out. A faster second buys more search, so the same program out-scores what the
  reference machine would give it. ALE-Bench has no software time-scale
  normalization, so the only comparable path is the reference CPU.
- **Toolchain.** The official judge compiles with gcc 15.2.0. These runs
  compiled with Apple clang. Different code generation, different solver speed.
- **Evaluation surface.** The agents optimized against 30 development cases; the
  rank is computed on 150 private ones.
- **A different task entirely, which is the deepest one.** Per its own paper,
  ShinkaEvolve's AHC039 result **warm-started from ALE-Agent's rank-5 solver**
  and refined it by about 2.3% over 50 generations, staying "algorithmically
  close" to that initialization. ALE-Agent itself generates around 1,000
  candidate programs by beam and best-first search. Mine starts from a single
  rectangle and takes ten iterations of one program improving itself. Even at
  matched hardware those are not the same experiment, and the honest sentence is
  "from-scratch values-driven self-improvement reached X" next to "ShinkaEvolve
  reached 3140 starting from ALE-Agent's solver". Not "beat".

The inflation is bounded, at least. A budget sweep shows these solvers plateau
at about 1.3 seconds of CPU per case, below the 2 second limit, with no cases
cut off by the wall clock. So they are not simply eating extra time. The effect
is real but it is not unbounded.

Two more things worth stating. The full ALE-Bench score averages Performance
over 40 problems, 10 in the Lite version; I ran one problem, so this is one
problem's Performance and not an ALE-Bench average. And my notes for this
mission already said, two days before the run, that a laptop number is not
C6i-normalized and is indicative rather than certified. The blunter version of
that rule, never claim state of the art from a laptop number, I only wrote down
the day after, once I had this number in front of me.

Certifying it would mean re-running the frozen solvers under the official
harness, same tester, same 150 private cases, gcc 15.2, C6i timing, then
matching ShinkaEvolve's protocol, then running multiple seeds. A cheaper
intermediate step, matched compiler plus a calibrated CPU budget plus a
score-versus-budget sweep, would replace "3390" with something honest of the
form "Performance X, as a band, at a C6i-equivalent budget". Until one of those
happens, 3390 is a number in my notes, not a result.

## What would have to be true for me to believe the main finding

**One run per cell.** Eight runs, eight cells, no replicates. That is
directional and not statistically powered, and I say it with some feeling,
because [yesterday I published a post](/blog/powered-replication/) about a
one-run-per-cell result of mine that a properly powered replication took apart.
Worse, that replication was about this exact variable: on a 2048 task, freezing
the values reproduced the whole capability gap, and self-modification added no
significant benefit on top of the disposition itself. So the honest status of
the interaction above is a hypothesis with a mechanism and a legible transcript,
not a finding.

**A version skew.** The nietzsche and tzeentch dispositions ran at generation v7
of the values files; anti and control exist only at v6. Each arrow in the figure
compares a run against its own twin at the same generation, so the pairs are
internally matched. What the skew clouds is exactly the comparison the headline
rests on: putting a v6 arrow next to a v7 arrow. It is documented and it was
unavoidable, and it is a reason to treat the ordering of the four arrows as
softer than it looks.

## The part I would keep

If it survives replication, the awkward part of this is that a self-limiting
specification is the hardest kind to notice from outside. The frozen `control`
run looks excellent in its logs. Careful hypotheses, honest reverts, no
overclaiming, every number checked. Nothing in that transcript says "this agent
is leaving 474 points on the table". The only thing that said so was the copy of
it that was allowed to disagree with its own instructions, and did.
