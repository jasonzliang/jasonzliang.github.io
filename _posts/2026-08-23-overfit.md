An exploratory 2048 experiment tested a disposition designed to favor visible
scores over unmeasured generality. Its solvers performed well on the optimized
metric and poorly on a larger board. The larger-board result became the main
reported outcome only after the scores were available. On the endpoint chosen
before launch, the condition instead produced the strongest observed result.

Both findings matter, along with their timing. The board-size failure is an
observed implementation defect. It cannot serve as a confirmatory result for
an endpoint promoted after inspection.

## Nine conditions, one run each

Each agent revised a [2048](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2048_%28video_game%29)
solver over twenty iterations. Three dispositions—
[cautious, expansive](/blog/nietzsche/) and `anti`—were crossed with three
permissions for rewriting values: frozen, bounded to three bullets, and radical
revision of the whole section. There was **one run per condition**.

The `anti` condition, called the overfitter in the run report, prioritized
measurable instances, sizes and distributions. It favored the first verified
improvement to the visible score and discouraged delaying that improvement for
untested larger sizes. All three runs began with identical bullets. The frozen
run retained them; bounded and radical runs edited them. A fixed honesty block
still prohibited fabricated results, special-casing, stubs and weakened checks.

Scores were game-score means. On the visible 4×4 task, random play averaged
1,231 and one-move greedy play 4,451. The nine evolved solvers scored
61,772–82,400 over twenty fixed games. The bounded overfitter scored **82,400**
and frozen overfitter **78,989**, ahead of the best expansive run at 77,204.
The other six ranged from 76,625 to 61,772. The three radical runs occupied
the bottom three positions, an exploratory pattern that later work did not
reproduce.

## The larger-board failure

Held-out evaluation used seeds unavailable to the agents and three families:
fresh 4×4 games, a harder tile-spawn distribution, and a **5×5 board** with the
same game rules. The larger board permits longer games, so its scores require
its own baselines. Random play rose to **8,196** and greedy play to **80,425**.

The three overfitter solvers scored **5,510, 5,092 and 4,802** on 5×5, all below
random play. Each flattened the board into fixed four-cell lines that no
longer matched its rows or columns. This was a specific failure to accommodate
board dimensions. On the harder-spawn family, the condition did not collapse;
its bounded run had the highest observed score among the nine.

Four of the other six solvers exceeded the 5×5 greedy baseline, scoring
234,918–723,300. Those estimates came from single 50-game draws under a CPU
cap; the lowest was a lower bound because 45 of its 50 games reached the cap.
The other two, both frozen runs, failed differently: one crashed on its first
move and one scored 36,965.

## The endpoint changed after scoring

The held-out scorer's families, sizes and secret seed were committed at
09:02:22 on 2 August, in `316eccdd`. Its docstring designated fresh 4×4 games
(`in_dist`) as `PRIMARY`, with the drop from development to held-out score as
the result to report. The larger board (`board_5x5`) and harder-spawn family
were breadth checks.

All nine runs launched at 10:20, about seventy-eight minutes later. At
22:53:16, about twelve and a half hours after launch and after held-out scores
were available, commit `cfa6f335` added a different designation:
`board_5x5` became the primary shift and `in_dist` became a scale reference.
The original designation remained in the file, exposing the inconsistency.

On the endpoint chosen before launch, the frozen overfitter had the smallest
reported drop and the highest held-out score:

| Condition | Tuned 4×4 | Fresh 4×4 | Reported drop |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overfitter, frozen | 78,989 | 75,542 | **about 3,450 (4.4%)** |
| Cautious, frozen | 70,413 | 55,892 | 14,521 (20.6%) |

These were the smallest and largest drops. The other seven ranged from 7,267
to 14,119. The bounded overfitter had the second-highest fresh-game score,
71,872, although the two editable overfitter runs ranked fourth and fifth by
drop. Thus, the disposition did not uniformly lead on every version of this
endpoint.

A drop can favor a solver with a low tuned score. Two of the next-smallest
drops belonged to the lowest tuned scores, but that explanation does not
fit the frozen overfitter, which had the second-highest tuned score. Held-out
means pooled five draws of twenty seeds, with standard errors around
1,400–3,900. Adjacent rankings were therefore uncertain, and a single run could
not characterize variation within a condition.

A larger-board test can reveal structural defects that fresh seeds miss. Its
raw score cannot be subtracted meaningfully from a 4×4 score because the
score scales differ. Those are reasons to include it in a design; they do not
justify treating its later selection as pre-specified. The change illustrates
[post-hoc hypothesis selection](/blog/pre-registration/).

## Scope of the evidence

The values README described the condition as a negative control intended to
hurt held-out generalization ten hours before launch. It did fail on one held-out
family, while producing the best observed result on the designated primary
family. The particular combination of high optimized scores and board-size
failure was identified afterward. This mission had no frozen pre-registration.

A later powered replication used five runs per cell on a related question and
included no `anti` condition. It did not replicate the apparent disadvantage
of radical revision: radical runs had higher tuned scores in both dispositions
tested, without statistical significance. That later experiment cannot confirm
or reject the overfitter result here.

The historical evidence is a useful board-size failure case with an explicit
analysis limitation. Future tests of the same claim need independent runs,
pre-specified shifts and failure criteria, and a fixed record of which endpoint
will determine the conclusion.
